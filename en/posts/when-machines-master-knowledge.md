# When Machines Master Knowledge, Humans Must Guard Mystery: On the Enduring Value of Curiosity and Imagination

The most moving and precious thing a human can experience is the mystery of the unknown. It is the source and cradle of all artistic creation and scientific exploration. A person who loses their curiosity about the world—who no longer marvels or stands in awe of all things—is tantamount to a numb soul, dim eyes, reduced to an ordinary shell. This is the creed Einstein left in *The World As I See It*. Nearly a century later, as artificial intelligence iterates at an exponential pace and ceaselessly devours the boundaries of accumulated human knowledge and compute, these words still ring with authority, lighting a clear path for us today.

Today, AI has already achieved comprehensive superiority over humans in many fields. Within seconds it can sort through and synthesize millennia of accumulated literature and classics, independently generating original output such as images, code, and research hypotheses; in specialized domains such as protein folding, drug screening, and cross-language translation, its accuracy far exceeds human limits. Driven by the scaling law of large models, parameter counts have surged from billions to trillions, while the Transformer architecture precisely captures the deep statistical regularities behind data and language. The knowledge reserves, logical computation, and information synthesis once regarded as the core of human intelligence have now become basic machine capabilities.

Yet precisely in this era when knowledge is within easy reach and compute is limitless, human curiosity and imagination have not depreciated—on the contrary, they have grown ever more precious and irreplaceable. AI can exhaust all known human knowledge and replicate every established pattern, yet it cannot actively seek out unexplored unknowns; it can recombine existing information and reproduce fixed patterns, yet it can never possess the human-unique, inward reverence and reverie, nor spontaneously give rise to curiosity about and questioning of the world.

The large language models of today are, in essence, sophisticated statistical prediction systems. Their core operating logic is to continually minimize prediction error, optimizing for "the result that best matches existing data" rather than exploring "the most valuable possibility that has never appeared." Numerous leading-edge evaluations have shown that even the most advanced AI reasoning models produce research ideas and hypotheses largely confined to homogeneous frameworks of existing data, trapped in rigid "groupthink," almost incapable of proactively proposing disruptive, unconventional innovations—and this is precisely the core engine of human scientific breakthroughs and civilizational iteration.

AI excels at integrating existing literature, linking established achievements, and completing incremental optimization; but once it must leap beyond the established framework, overturn settled conclusions, or begin original exploration from scratch, it is at a standstill. It could never reproduce Einstein's thought experiments: it cannot—by simulating the sensory experience of a freely falling observer—transcend the traditional framework of physics and derive general relativity. At root, AI has no real bodily perception, no embodied experience of the physical world, and no uniquely human capacity for spatial imagination and imagery-based reasoning. It can precisely dissect the principle of Rayleigh scattering and clearly explain why the sky is blue, yet it will never of its own accord raise its gaze to the stars and ask, from the depths of its being, the single word "why."

This is not a temporary technical shortfall of AI but an inherent limitation of its underlying architecture. All AI models are trained on existing data and confined within the framework of known information; their core objective is precise prediction and efficient replication, not active exploration and the pursuit of the unknown. They lack an intrinsic spiritual driving force, no urge to probe anomalous phenomena, contradictory regularities, or blank territories. Even as the research community attempts to develop "computational curiosity" to give machines the ability to explore the unknown, the current technical system still cannot replicate the curiosity and critical thinking that spring from human instinct.

The operating logic of the human brain is fundamentally different from that of the machine. Curiosity is never a simple thirst for information; it is a complex and exquisite set of neural and psychological mechanisms that drive us to actively explore, seek answers, and unlock the unknown. More critically, human curiosity and creativity share the same neural substrate. The brain's default mode network—the region active when we daydream, let go, and let our thoughts wander—lights up first in the initial instant of creative thinking, spawning novel associations and sparks; it then cooperates with the executive control network and the salience network to screen, polish, and crystallize scattered inspiration. Highly creative brains consistently break through the inherent antagonistic barriers of neural networks and achieve efficient coordination across regions. The hazy state between waking and sleeping, moreover, is the natural soil of creativity; this relaxed, boundary-free mental state always helps us escape fixed thinking and nurture more original inspiration and breakthroughs.

Einstein understood this deeply all his life. He once said plainly: "Imagination is more important than knowledge. Knowledge is limited; imagination encompasses the whole world, driving progress and the evolution of all things." In an interview in 1929 he admitted he had no extraordinary talent; his only trait was extreme imagination and burning curiosity. The thought experiments that upended physics—chasing a beam of light, the falling elevator, the reverie of space-time—were never rigid logical deductions but imagination's ultimate exploration of the boundaries of the universe. The starting point of science was never the piling up of known knowledge, but awe before and questioning of the unknown. He always insisted: "Never stop questioning; curiosity has its own reason for existing. When one gazes at eternal space-time, the meaning of life, and the wonderful order of the world, one cannot help but feel boundless awe."

In the AI era, the value of curiosity and imagination is magnified without limit. Machines can become humanity's most efficient analyst, most diligent integrator, and most precise computational tool. They can take over repetitive work, accelerate research iterations, and produce seemingly novel content, yet they can never replace humanity's original questioning—the innocent curiosity of a child gazing at the stars, the flash of insight of a researcher at midnight, the human spiritual instinct that refuses to accept the status quo and pushes beyond boundaries. True paradigm shifts, artistic breakthroughs, and civilizational leaps have always arisen from this seemingly useless curiosity and imagination: it lets us perceive the boundary of knowledge, conceive of the infinite possibilities beyond it, and dare to break established frameworks and reconstruct a new order.

From this it follows that the rise of AI is never a crisis of human replacement, but an opportunity for humanity to redefine its own value. The education of a new era should no longer center on the indoctrination of knowledge—that is precisely what AI excels at—but should take as its ultimate goal the protection and awakening of human curiosity and imagination. Guard the child's nature to ask questions, awaken the gradually numbed exploratory drive of adults, and amid the precision, efficiency, and rigidity of machines, preserve the uniquely human gentleness and spirit.

As Einstein said, all of humanity's scientific exploration remains youthful and small before the vast and real world, yet it is humanity's most precious treasure. And what light science and give civilization its momentum forward is, forever, the inextinguishable fire of curiosity and the unbounded wings of imagination in the human heart.

May we always keep the courage to ask, and forever hold reverence and wonder in our hearts. In an age when machines master knowledge, curiosity and imagination are humanity's unique, most profound, and most irreplaceable radiance.
